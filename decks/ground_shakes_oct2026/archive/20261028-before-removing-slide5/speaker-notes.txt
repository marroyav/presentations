Ground shakes — speaker notes, 28 October 2026

1. Ground shakes
We reviewed seven runs using charge, PDS and slow-control data. The charge
precursor and electrical pickup were already shown in the EDMS slides. Here
we ask how the early pulses relate to the precursor and the main shake, and
whether any charge signals could belong to those pulses.

2. Before the main shake
Read the plots from top to bottom. Two PDS pulses appear first, at about -183
and -161 microseconds. The small TPC precursor rises later. The main shake is
at zero. The upper plots are magnified; the main shake is much larger.
This example shows the order of the signals. It does not establish what
causes them. PDS records both light and electrical pickup.

3. Double pulses
These two events have pulse gaps of about 23 and 66 microseconds. We found
at least two early pulses in 101 of 716 run 32731 events. The search used the
window from 100 to 300 microseconds before the main shake.
Different gaps do not rule out one source. A source can fire twice at
different intervals while keeping the same light-to-charge drift delay.
We have not yet established whether these pulses are light.

4. Timing of the early PDS pulse
This is the histogram Manuel asked for: PDS pulse time minus main-shake time.
We removed the old 100-300 microsecond cut. For each event, we take the largest
sharp pulse before the small TPC precursor. There are 237 events in run 32731
and 167 in run 32739. Their median times are 230 and 220 microseconds before
the shake. The full range is about 73-316 microseconds before.
So the two runs cover a similar broad interval. They do not give one narrow
delay in this selected sample. Fifty-one of the 404 values fall outside the
old 100-300 microsecond window. Taking the first pulse instead still gives
a broad spread, with medians about 239 and 238 microseconds before the shake.
This is timing relative to the main shake. We must match actual charge to a
light pulse before interpreting a delay as drift distance.

Histogram details, if asked
The PDS search finds sharp negative edges on at least 21 channels of DAPHNE
109. It searches the wider recorded window, from -4,000 to -20 microseconds.
The precursor time is the existing APA1 two-ADC rise sustained for five bins;
it is a readout marker, not an exact physical start. Largest means the
largest median leading-edge change on recorded connected channels. It is
not a measurement of photon yield. Of 1,378 November charge candidates,
1,348 have a usable precursor time, 407 have an earlier pulse, and 404 have
enough samples to rank pulse size. Missing packets and pulse thresholds can
hide earlier activity. Some channels have samples before -320 microseconds,
so that is not a hard recording boundary. Full definitions are in SOURCES.md.

5. This charge signal starts before both PDS pulses
Each row is a collection wire. The diagonal signal is already visible at
-331 microseconds; the PDS pulses follow at -183 and -161. If this is one
charge segment and our relative timing is right, neither later pulse can be
the light from the interaction that produced its earlier part.
This is one particular assignment. Other later charge can still match, and
repeated interactions at the same place remain possible. We still need a
3D fit: a feature in one wire view could combine more than one interaction.

6. A repeated source is still possible
Blue pairs charge with PDS pulses from the same event. Orange keeps the
charge but borrows PDS pulses from another event in the same run and file.
Both have a broad peak near 325 microseconds.
That does not rule out a real source. If repeated interactions happen at the
same place, their light-to-charge delays can be similar. Swapping events can
keep that timing pattern, because we align them all to the main shake.
My earlier wording overstated what this comparison could tell us. We need
charge positions and corrected drift delays to distinguish a repeated source
from electrical pickup or an effect of the recording windows.
A drift delay gives information along the drift direction. It does not, by
itself, identify a single point in the cryostat.

7. Electrical pickup in the PDS
The EDMS slides already show signals on disconnected channels. We use those
channels to predict part of the connected-channel signal. We fit on run
32731 and test on run 32739. Signals from the same event work better than
signals borrowed from another event: errors are about 92% and 102%.
Here 100% means predicting no signal change. The improvement is partial and
varies by channel. This quantifies known pickup; it does not tell us which
board, cable or physical process started it.

8. Slow controls
We checked whether 32 monitors follow three PDS and TPC measurements in the
two November runs. No correlation passes our check in both runs with
one-minute bins. A weak current hint changes when we change the bins.
This does not overturn the earlier HV or wire-bias observations. We have not
repeated the study of shake rate versus HV or the bias-off comparison. Slow
sampling can also miss a short transient. Two ground monitors have too few
samples for the comparison.

9. Next checks
First, fit the charge tracks and see whether different events point to the
same place. Check whether the light-to-charge delay agrees with that position.
For double pulses, look for two charge responses with the same gap and a
common drift delay. We should also pass a repeated-source model through our
actual recording windows before using event mixing to reject it.
Revisit the wire-bias change in runs 30201 and 30202. Finally, compare with
events without shakes, under similar conditions, to find what is distinctive.

Backup: the small TPC precursor
The earlier clock test has errors of 66.1 versus 33.4 microseconds on 225
unused records. It times the small signal shared across the APA, not charge
arriving from one location. It therefore does not test a fixed drift distance.
The amplitude test uses 477 records and improves the size estimate by 4.1%.
